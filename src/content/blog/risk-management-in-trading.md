Risk management determines how much a trade can affect your account.

A trading strategy tells you **when to enter and exit**. Risk management determines **how much capital you expose to that decision**.

That distinction matters because a profitable strategy can still produce damaging results when positions are too large, losses are allowed to expand, or several trades create more exposure than the trader intended.

Risk management does not make an unprofitable strategy profitable. It controls what happens when the strategy is wrong.

## Risk Starts Before the Trade

Before entering a position, three things should already be clear:

- Where is the trade invalidated?
    
- How much are you willing to lose if that happens?
    
- What position size keeps the potential loss within that limit?
    

These variables are connected.

A wider stop does not automatically mean greater monetary risk. If the trade requires more room before it is invalidated, position size can be reduced so that the amount at risk remains within the same limit.

That is why position size should be calculated from the trade's structure rather than chosen independently.

The same principle applies across instruments. A fixed position size can represent very different levels of risk depending on price behaviour, contract specifications and volatility.

The number of units tells you how large the position is. It does not, by itself, tell you how much the position can cost.

## Risk Does Not End at Entry

A stop-loss controls what happens when a trade moves against you. Managing a position after it moves in your favour is a separate problem.

Some systems leave the position unchanged until the original stop or target is reached. Others use partial exits, break-even adjustments or trailing stops.

None of these is automatically correct.

What matters is whether the action belongs to the trading system.

Moving a stop because a losing trade feels uncomfortable is not the same as following a predefined exit rule. Moving a stop to break even because you do not want to give back unrealised profit is not automatically risk management either. It is a trading decision, and it needs a rule behind it.

The same principle applies to winning trades.

A profitable position is still exposed to price movement until it is closed. An unrealised gain is an outcome in progress, not capital that has already been secured.

## Trading Style Changes the Risk

The amount of attention a position requires, the time it remains exposed and the types of execution risk it faces all depend partly on the trading style.

### Scalping

Scalping involves opening and closing positions over short periods to capture relatively small price movements.

That makes execution particularly important. Spread, commissions, slippage and liquidity can have a greater effect when the expected move is small.

A scalper also needs to consider cumulative exposure. Ten trades risking a small amount each are not necessarily low risk if they are all taken under the same market conditions or in highly correlated instruments.

The individual trades may look small on paper while the combined exposure tells a different story.

### Day Trading

Day traders generally open and close positions within the same trading day.

Because positions have more time to move than a typical scalp, stop placement and position size become more important. The trader also needs to account for the possibility of several losing trades during the same session.

Positions are normally closed before the day ends, so overnight price gaps are less relevant than they are for longer-term positions.

That does not remove risk. It changes which risks deserve the most attention.

### Swing Trading

Swing traders hold positions for several days or longer to capture larger price movements.

That can require a wider stop because the trade has to withstand normal fluctuations on the chosen timeframe. A wider stop can be paired with a smaller position so that the amount of capital at risk remains within the trader's limit.

Longer holding periods introduce other considerations as well. News can occur while the position is open, market conditions can change, and price can move significantly while the trader is away from the screen.

A position can therefore be well-sized and still face risks that have little to do with its original entry.

The risk-management process has to account for those conditions rather than assume every trade behaves the same way.

## Market Conditions Can Change the Risk

A stop-loss defines the price at which you intend to exit. It does not guarantee that the trade will be executed at exactly that price.

When markets move quickly, the next available execution price can differ from the stop level. The difference is slippage.

That matters during high-impact economic releases and other periods of rapid price movement. A trader who normally expects a certain loss from a stop may experience a larger loss when execution conditions change.

A stop is therefore a planned exit level, not a promise that the market will fill the order at that exact price.

This does not make trading during news inherently wrong. It means the trader has to decide whether the strategy is designed to operate under those conditions and whether the additional execution risk is acceptable.

## The Instrument Matters

The same numerical stop distance does not carry the same meaning across markets.

A 30-pip stop on EUR/USD and a 30-point movement on gold are not equivalent measures of risk. The instruments have different price behaviour, conventions and volatility.

The better question is not whether a stop is a particular number of pips or points.

It is whether the stop gives the trade enough room for its normal price movement while allowing the resulting position size to stay within the trader's risk limit.

That is why stop distance and position size should be considered together.

The stop defines how far the trade can move against you before the setup is invalidated. Position size determines how much that movement costs the account.

## Your Signal Cannot Determine Your Risk

A trading signal can give you an entry, a stop-loss and a take-profit.

It cannot determine how much of your own account you should risk.

That depends on the size of your account, your trading rules and the amount of capital you are prepared to lose on one trade or across a sequence of trades.

Two traders can receive the same signal and use different position sizes without either of them misunderstanding the setup.

The signal defines the trade. The trader decides how much capital to allocate to it.

This distinction matters because a technically identical setup can produce very different account-level consequences depending on position size.

## Risk Management Does Not Create an Edge

This is where risk management is often misunderstood.

A strategy with negative expectancy can still lose money when every trade is correctly sized and every stop is respected.

Risk management limits the consequences of the strategy's results. It does not improve the underlying probability of those results.

Its role is narrower and more practical.

It can keep one trade from taking too much from the account. It can limit the effect of losing streaks. It can keep position size consistent. It can also reduce the need to improvise risk decisions while a trade is moving against you.

Those are process benefits, not a promise of profit.

A disciplined loss is still a loss. Risk management changes its size and its place in the larger sequence of trades; it does not change the direction of the market.

## A Practical Framework

Before entering a trade, you should be able to answer:

**Where is the trade invalidated?**

**How much am I willing to lose if that happens?**

**What position size keeps that loss within my limit?**

**Does the instrument's normal volatility support the stop distance?**

**Could current market conditions increase execution risk?**

**What is the rule for managing the position if it moves in my favour?**

**What happens to my overall exposure if several trades are open at once?**

These questions do not tell you whether a trade will win.

They determine how much the outcome can matter if it does not.

That is the practical purpose of risk management: not to remove uncertainty from trading, but to define how much of that uncertainty your account is allowed to absorb.
