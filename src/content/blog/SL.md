# Why Your Stop Loss Doesn't Always Protect You

A stop loss can define the loss you intend to take.

It cannot guarantee the price at which your trade will close.

Two things can change the outcome: the market may execute your stop at a different price, or you may change the stop after entering the trade.

## 1. Your Stop May Not Execute at Its Exact Price

A stop loss tells your broker where to trigger an exit. It does not guarantee that the position will be filled at that exact price.

The difference between the price you intended to exit at and the price you actually receive is called **slippage**.

Suppose you buy an asset at $100 and place a stop at $95.

If the market reaches $95 and there is sufficient liquidity, the position may be closed near that level.

But markets can move quickly during major news, periods of high volatility, or when liquidity is limited. If there are no available orders at $95 when your stop triggers, the position may be filled at the next available price.

If that price is $93, your loss is $7 rather than the $5 you originally planned for.

The stop loss still worked as an exit mechanism. It simply did not control the execution price.

**A stop loss is an exit instruction, not a guaranteed fill.**

## 2. You May Move the Stop

The second problem has nothing to do with execution.

It is a change to the trade itself.

Suppose you enter a position with a stop at the level where your original setup becomes invalid. The market moves toward that stop, and instead of accepting the planned loss, you move it further away.

The market moves against you again.

You move it again.

At that point, the trade can carry substantially more risk than it did when you entered.

The stop loss did not fail. **The risk boundary moved.**

That distinction matters because setting a stop and respecting the stop are two different decisions.

## What Actually Determines Your Risk?

A stop loss is only one part of the risk calculation.

Before entering a trade, you need to know:

**How much are you willing to lose?**

**At what price is the trade no longer valid?**

**What position size keeps the potential loss within your risk limit?**

**Could current market conditions affect execution?**

The stop then provides a predefined exit based on those decisions.

It does not determine your risk by itself.

If you move the stop because you do not want to realize the loss, the amount you originally agreed to risk is no longer the amount you are actually risking.

## A Stop Loss Is Not a Guarantee

A stop loss does not make a losing trade harmless.

It gives you a predefined exit point, while actual execution can still depend on market conditions. And it only reflects your original risk plan if you leave it where you placed it.

You cannot control whether a trade works.

You can control how much you are willing to risk, where the trade becomes invalid, how large your position is, and whether you follow that plan after entering.

**The stop loss is the mechanism. The risk decision is yours.**
