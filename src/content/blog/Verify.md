A win rate tells you what happened inside a provider's chosen sample.

It does not tell you whether the sample is complete, independently verifiable, or representative of the provider's actual trading record.

A win rate without the underlying trades is a little like a receipt with the line items missing. You have a total. You do not yet know what produced it.

So the first question is not:

> **“What is your win rate?”**

It is:

> **“Can I verify the trades behind it, including the losing ones?”**

## What to Check in a Track Record

|Check|What it tells you|What its absence tells you|
|---|---|---|
|Losses posted in the same channel as wins, on the same day|The record includes both outcomes rather than selected trades|You cannot tell whether losing trades were omitted|
|Timestamps that can be checked against market data|The stated entry or exit could have occurred at the reported time and price|You cannot independently check the published trade|
|A sample large enough for the provider's trading frequency|The results cover enough trades to show how the method behaved over a meaningful period|A short run may tell you very little about the method|
|Reward-to-risk shown alongside win rate|You can assess the relationship between winning and losing trades|Win rate alone does not show whether the strategy was profitable|

The last point matters because win rate and profitability are different measurements.

A provider can win 70% of its trades and still lose money if the losing trades are large relative to the winners. A provider with a lower win rate can be profitable when its average winning trade is sufficiently large relative to its average loss.

The number is only part of the record. The relationship between the numbers matters.

## Start With the Losing Trades

A list of winning calls is easy to present.

That makes losing trades more informative.

Look for whether losses appear in the same place, under the same reporting process, and with the same level of detail as winning trades. A useful record should let you follow a trade from the original signal through to its outcome rather than asking you to rely on a retrospective summary.

Then check when the trade was called.

A timestamp gives you something concrete to compare against independent market data. If a provider reports a EUR/USD entry at a particular price and time, you can check whether the market was trading at or near that level at the stated time.

That does not establish that every subscriber received or executed the trade at that exact price. It does establish whether the published signal corresponds to a market condition that existed when the provider said it did.

## A Clean Track Record Still Has Limits

Verification answers one question:

> **Did these trades happen as reported?**

It does not answer another:

> **Will the same results continue?**

Even a complete and verifiable record can contain periods of unusually strong or weak performance. Market conditions change, and a strategy can behave differently as volatility, liquidity, or broader market structure changes.

A track record is therefore evidence of what happened during a defined period. It is not a forecast of what happens next.

That distinction matters when evaluating historical performance. Verification can reduce uncertainty about the record itself. It cannot remove the uncertainty inherent in future trading results.

## What to Check Before You Pay

You do not need to audit every trade before subscribing. A small number of checks can establish whether the published record is worth examining further.

**Look at recent losing trades.** Do not review only the provider's strongest period.

**Check a timestamp.** Compare at least one published entry or exit with independent market data.

**Check the reward-to-risk relationship.** A win rate without the size of wins and losses is incomplete.

**Check the sample.** A few trades show what happened. A larger sample gives you more information about how the method behaved over time.

Then keep checking the record after you subscribe.

The process you were shown before payment should remain consistent with what is being reported afterward.

The useful question is not whether a provider can show you a good number.

It is whether you can inspect the trades that produced it.
